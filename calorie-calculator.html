An estimate, not a prescription. Mifflin-St Jeor predicts resting energy needs for the average healthy adult within about 10% — individual metabolism, body composition, and medical conditions can shift the real number either way. The ~3,500-calorie-per-pound rule used for the goal targets above is a widely-used estimate, not an exact law: real-world weight change also involves water retention and metabolic adaptation. For a plan tailored to you, a registered dietitian or doctor is the right call.

Worked example

A 30-year-old woman, 165 cm tall, 65 kg, moderately active: BMR = 10×65 + 6.25×165 − 5×30 − 161 = 650 + 1,031.25 − 150 − 161 ≈ 1,370 kcal/day. TDEE = 1,370 × 1.55 (moderate activity) ≈ 2,124 kcal/day. To lose weight steadily, her target would be about 2,124 − 250 ≈ 1,874 kcal/day — comfortably above the 1,200 kcal/day floor this tool checks for.

Frequently asked questions

Why does activity level change the result so much?

TDEE = BMR × an activity multiplier (1.2 for sedentary up to 1.9 for extra active), so the same body can have a "maintenance" calorie number that swings by 50–60% just based on how much you move — a bigger effect than most individual food choices.

Is Mifflin-St Jeor more accurate than older formulas like Harris-Benedict?

For most healthy adults, yes — Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) is the equation current clinical nutrition guidelines generally recommend over the older Harris-Benedict revisions, because it was validated against more accurate measured data. No formula is exact for any one individual; both are population-average estimates.

Why did I get a warning that my calorie target is "too low"?

This tool flags any target below roughly 1,200 kcal/day (women) or 1,500 kcal/day (men) — thresholds commonly cited by health authorities like the NHS and Mayo Clinic as the point where an unsupervised diet risks inadequate nutrition. It's a population-level guideline, not a personal medical judgment — if you need to go lower, that's a conversation for a doctor or registered dietitian.

Why is the "faster" weight-loss target exactly 500 calories below maintenance?

It's built on the widely-used rule of thumb that roughly 3,500 kcal of deficit corresponds to about 1 lb (0.45 kg) of fat loss, so a 500 kcal/day deficit ≈ 1 lb/week. It's a useful planning estimate, not an exact physiological law — actual results vary with water retention and metabolic adaptation over time.

Does this work for pregnant women, children, or people with a diagnosed medical condition?

No. Mifflin-St Jeor and the activity multipliers here are validated for generally healthy, non-pregnant adults. Pregnancy, growth, and many medical conditions change energy needs in ways this calculator doesn't account for — a doctor or registered dietitian should guide those numbers instead.
