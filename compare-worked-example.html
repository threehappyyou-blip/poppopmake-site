Same Salary Number, Three Very Different Paychecks

An 80,000 salary offer sounds like the same offer wherever it's made. Run it through three real systems and the share you actually keep moves by ten points — without anyone changing the number on the offer letter.

The setup

Same nominal number — 80,000 — in three places, single filer, no pre-tax deductions, native currency each time (no exchange rate applied, exactly like our own comparison tool):

Texas, USA

$80,000 salary
$65,110
81.4% kept

Ontario, Canada

$80,000 salary
$59,027
73.8% kept

England

£80,000 salary
£56,957
71.2% kept

Texas keeps the largest share partly because it has no state income tax at all — the only tax at that level is federal plus FICA. Ontario adds a full second layer (federal plus provincial tax, CPP2, and EI) on top of a very similar federal structure. England's system front-loads more of the burden at this income level through Income Tax's 40% higher-rate band, which starts at £37,700 — well below where 80,000's equivalent band starts in the other two systems.

Why the raw number alone is misleading

If you're comparing job offers, cost-of-living adjustments, or just curious how far a number would actually go somewhere else, the salary figure by itself tells you less than it seems to. A 10-percentage-point swing in what you actually keep, on the identical stated salary, is the kind of gap that changes whether a move is a raise or a pay cut in practice.

This is deliberately not a currency-converted comparison — see our note on why we don't do currency conversion on the comparison tool itself. The point here isn't "which country pays better" (that depends on exchange rates and cost of living, which this doesn't touch), just how differently the same nominal number gets treated by three real tax systems.
