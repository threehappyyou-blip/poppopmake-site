How Paycheck Taxes Are Calculated (2026)

Updated November 2026 for 2026 tax rules

Paycheck taxes are calculated in a fixed order: your employer takes pre-tax deductions off your gross pay, applies Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) to what’s left, then withholds federal and state income tax on your taxable wages using your W-4. Here’s each step for 2026 — and one real paycheck worked through to the cent.

Key facts (2026)

  • Order: gross pay → pre-tax deductions → FICA → federal income tax → state/local tax → after-tax deductions → net pay.
  • Social Security: 6.2% of wages up to $184,500; Medicare: 1.45% of all wages, plus 0.9% above $200,000.
  • Federal income tax: 7 brackets from 10% to 37%, after a $16,100 standard deduction (single) or $32,200 (married filing jointly).
  • Pre-tax health premiums and HSA lower both FICA and income tax; a traditional 401(k) lowers income tax but not FICA.
  • Example: a $65,000 single filer in Illinois, paid biweekly, keeps $1,801.00 of a $2,500 paycheck after a 5% 401(k) and a health premium.

If you've ever looked at your offer letter and then your first pay stub and wondered where a fifth of your money went, you're not imagining it. A $65,000 salary in the US doesn't arrive as $65,000 — it passes through three separate layers of tax before it reaches your bank account, and each layer works differently.

Layer 1: Federal income tax

This is the one most people have heard of, and the one most people misunderstand. Federal tax doesn't take one flat percentage of your whole salary — it works in slices. For 2026, there are seven slices (the IRS calls them brackets): 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%. Your first dollars are taxed at 10%, the next chunk at 12%, and so on, only reaching the higher rates once you've earned enough to get there.

Before any of that math happens, you subtract the standard deduction — $16,100 for a single filer in 2026, $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly. That comes off the top first, so a $65,000 salary is actually taxed as if it were $48,900 (for a single filer). This is also why your effective rate (what you actually pay, on average) is always lower than your marginal rate (the rate on your last dollar earned) — a distinction that trips up a lot of first-time earners.

Layer 2: FICA (Social Security and Medicare)

FICA is really two taxes wearing one name. Social Security takes 6.2% of your wages, but only up to a cap — $184,500 for 2026. Earn more than that in a year, and Social Security tax stops for the rest of the year (though it starts again in January). Medicare takes 1.45% of every dollar you earn, with no cap at all, plus an extra 0.9% once you cross $200,000 (or $250,000 for married couples filing jointly).

Unlike federal tax, FICA isn't progressive and doesn't care about a standard deduction — it's a flat percentage of your gross wages from the first dollar.

Layer 3: State tax (the one that varies the most)

This is where two people with identical salaries can end up with very different paychecks, purely based on where they live. Nine states — including Texas, Florida, and Washington — charge no state income tax at all. Fifteen use a single flat rate, from Arizona's 2.5% up to Idaho's 5.3% (Pennsylvania's is 3.07%, Colorado's 4.4%, Illinois's 4.95%). The rest — California and New York among them — use their own progressive bracket systems that can rival the federal one in complexity.

Some states also take small payroll premiums for disability, paid family leave or long-term-care insurance — California's 1.3% State Disability Insurance and Washington's WA Cares are two examples. They aren't income tax, but they do come out of your paycheck, and on a pay stub they show up as their own lines.

Why this matters for your actual paycheck

Your pay stub also depends on how often you're paid. A $65,000 salary paid biweekly (26 times a year) splits differently than the same salary paid semi-monthly (24 times a year) — the annual totals are identical, but the per-paycheck amount isn't, since 26 doesn't divide evenly into most numbers the way 24 does. None of this is a mistake on your employer's part; it's just arithmetic.

It's also worth knowing that none of the three layers above account for things like 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, or HSA contributions — those typically come out before federal tax is calculated, which is part of why contributing to a 401(k) can lower your visible tax bill even though the money isn't gone, just deferred.

Worked example: one $65,000 paycheck, step by step

Here is a single filer earning $65,000 in Illinois, paid every two weeks (26 paychecks), putting 5% into a traditional 401(k) and paying $2,400 a year for employer health insurance through a pre-tax (Section 125) plan.

5. Medicare1.45% × $2,407.69−$34.91
6. Federal income taxOn $2,282.69 of taxable wages; for the year: $59,350 − $16,100 standard deduction = $43,250, taxed 10% on the first $12,400 and 12% on the rest = $4,942 ÷ 26−$190.08

Over the year that’s $12,524 in taxes, an effective rate of 19.3% of gross pay, even though this worker’s top federal bracket is 12%. The same salary in Texas, with no 401(k) or health premium, takes home $2,092.60 per paycheck — no state income tax, but also no tax-free savings.

Your employer’s payroll system uses the IRS percentage-method tables (Publication 15-T) on each paycheck, so actual withholding can differ from these annual-based figures by a few dollars per check. The total settles when you file your return.

How your W-4 changes the federal tax line

Your W-4 doesn’t change how much tax you owe; it changes how much is withheld along the way. Step 1 (filing status) picks the bracket table, Step 2 (multiple jobs or a working spouse) withholds more so two incomes don’t each get a full standard deduction, Step 3 (dependents) lowers withholding by the child and dependent credits, and Step 4 lets you add other income, extra deductions or a flat extra amount per paycheck. If you got a big refund last year, you’re withholding more than you need to; if you owed, add an amount in Step 4(c).

Frequently asked questions

How are paycheck taxes calculated?

Your employer starts from gross pay, subtracts pre-tax deductions such as health premiums and 401(k) contributions, takes 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare, then withholds federal and state income tax on the remaining taxable wages using your W-4 and the IRS tables. What’s left after any after-tax deductions is your net pay.

What percentage of my paycheck goes to taxes?

For most US workers it’s roughly 15–30% of gross pay. FICA alone is 7.65%. In the example above, a $65,000 earner in Illinois pays about 19.3% in total; the same salary in a state with no income tax pays closer to 16%.

Why is federal tax withheld if I’m in the 12% bracket but pay less than 12%?

Brackets apply in slices. Only income above $12,400 of taxable income (single, 2026) is taxed at 12%; the first slice is taxed at 10%, and the $16,100 standard deduction isn’t taxed at all. Your effective federal rate is therefore much lower than your top bracket.

Does a 401(k) lower Social Security and Medicare tax?

No. Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce federal (and most state) income tax, but Social Security and Medicare are still charged on them. Pre-tax health premiums and payroll HSA contributions reduce both.

Why does my paycheck change in January?

New tax-year amounts start: brackets, the standard deduction and the Social Security wage base all change, and if you passed $184,500 in wages last year, Social Security tax starts again in January. Benefit premiums often change on January 1 too.
