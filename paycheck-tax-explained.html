How Your Paycheck Tax Actually Works

Updated for 2026 tax rules

Your salary is one number. Your paycheck is a smaller one. Here's exactly where the difference goes, in the order it actually gets taken out.

If you've ever looked at your offer letter and then your first pay stub and wondered where a fifth of your money went, you're not imagining it. A $65,000 salary in the US doesn't arrive as $65,000 — it passes through three separate layers of tax before it reaches your bank account, and each layer works differently.

Layer 1: Federal income tax

This is the one most people have heard of, and the one most people misunderstand. Federal tax doesn't take one flat percentage of your whole salary — it works in slices. For 2026, there are seven slices (the IRS calls them brackets): 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%. Your first dollars are taxed at 10%, the next chunk at 12%, and so on, only reaching the higher rates once you've earned enough to get there.

Before any of that math happens, you subtract the standard deduction — $16,100 for a single filer in 2026, $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly. That comes off the top first, so a $65,000 salary is actually taxed as if it were $48,900 (for a single filer). This is also why your effective rate (what you actually pay, on average) is always lower than your marginal rate (the rate on your last dollar earned) — a distinction that trips up a lot of first-time earners.

Layer 2: FICA (Social Security and Medicare)

FICA is really two taxes wearing one name. Social Security takes 6.2% of your wages, but only up to a cap — $184,500 for 2026. Earn more than that in a year, and Social Security tax stops for the rest of the year (though it starts again in January). Medicare takes 1.45% of every dollar you earn, with no cap at all, plus an extra 0.9% once you cross $200,000 (or $250,000 for married couples filing jointly).

Unlike federal tax, FICA isn't progressive and doesn't care about a standard deduction — it's a flat percentage of your gross wages from the first dollar.

Layer 3: State tax (the one that varies the most)

This is where two people with identical salaries can end up with very different paychecks, purely based on where they live. Nine states — including Texas, Florida, and Washington — charge no state income tax at all. Others use a single flat rate (Pennsylvania's 3.07% is the lowest of the states we cover; Colorado and Illinois also use flat rates). A handful of states, including California and New York, use their own progressive bracket systems that can rival the federal one in complexity.

Why this matters for your actual paycheck

Your pay stub also depends on how often you're paid. A $65,000 salary paid biweekly (26 times a year) splits differently than the same salary paid semi-monthly (24 times a year) — the annual totals are identical, but the per-paycheck amount isn't, since 26 doesn't divide evenly into most numbers the way 24 does. None of this is a mistake on your employer's part; it's just arithmetic.

It's also worth knowing that none of the three layers above account for things like 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, or HSA contributions — those typically come out before federal tax is calculated, which is part of why contributing to a 401(k) can lower your visible tax bill even though the money isn't gone, just deferred.
