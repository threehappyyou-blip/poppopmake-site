Why We Don't Claim Our AI Checker Is 99% Accurate

A lot of AI-detection tools advertise near-perfect accuracy. The evidence for that claim doesn't hold up well, and the people most likely to get hurt by it aren't cheaters — they're just people who don't write in the detector's preferred style.

What the detectors actually measure

Most AI-writing detectors score text using something called "perplexity" — roughly, how predictable each word is given the words before it. AI-generated text tends to be low-perplexity: smooth, statistically average, few surprises. So detectors flag low-perplexity writing as likely AI.

The problem is that a lot of very human writing is also low-perplexity — simpler vocabulary, more standard sentence structures, formulaic transitions. That describes a lot of writing by people who learned English as a second language, among others.

Stanford HAI, testing seven commercial detectors on essays written entirely by humans before ChatGPT existed. Native-English-speaker essays saw a far lower false-positive rate from the same tools.

Every one of those essays was human-written. Some detectors flagged the same essay unanimously. The researchers' explanation matches the mechanism above: non-native speakers often score lower on the exact complexity measures detectors use as a proxy for "human," so the tool mistakes a learner's natural writing style for a machine's.

What universities did after finding this out

This isn't a fringe concern. In August 2023, Vanderbilt University disabled Turnitin's AI detector for its entire student body, pointing to both the false-positive risk and the documented bias against non-native English writers. The math they published was blunt: even at a claimed 1% false-positive rate, Vanderbilt's roughly 75,000 annual paper submissions meant hundreds of students could be wrongly flagged every year.

Vanderbilt wasn't alone for long. Over the following two years, MIT, Yale, the University of Pittsburgh, Northwestern, and dozens of other institutions restricted or dropped automated AI detection for grading and disciplinary decisions, most citing the same combination of unreliable accuracy claims and disproportionate harm to specific groups of students.

So what does that mean for a tool like ours?

It means we didn't build a classifier and we're not going to call it one. The Writing & AI tool on this site flags specific, common AI-writing habits — certain stock phrases, some overused transitional patterns, a few structural tics — and suggests plainer alternatives. It's an editing aid, not a verdict. It won't tell you a piece of writing "is" AI-generated, because that's a claim the underlying science doesn't reliably support for anyone's writing, and least reliably for exactly the writers who'd be hurt most by getting it wrong.

If you're a non-native English speaker, a technical writer, or anyone whose natural style leans toward simpler, more consistent sentence patterns, that's exactly the profile the research says gets misjudged by detectors that claim otherwise. We'd rather tell you that upfront than sell you a number we can't back up.
