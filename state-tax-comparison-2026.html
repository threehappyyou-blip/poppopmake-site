9 States Compared: Where Your Paycheck Goes Furthest

2026 tax year · recalculated October 2026

Same salary, same job, same filing status — and yet a $65,000 earner in Texas takes home about $3,200 more per year than the exact same earner in New York. Here's the real math, state by state.

Federal tax and FICA are the same no matter where you live. State tax is the wildcard, and it varies more than most people expect — from nothing at all to a genuinely complex bracket system layered with its own extra payroll taxes. Below are nine states that cover the whole range, ranked from lightest to heaviest tax burden, using a single filer earning $65,000 as the benchmark. No state income tax: Texas, Florida, Washington

None of these three taxes wages as income, but they aren't identical. Texas and Florida add nothing at all: a $65,000 single filer keeps $54,407 after federal tax and FICA — a 16.3% total effective rate, the lowest of the nine. Washington has no income tax either, but it does withhold two small payroll premiums: WA Cares (0.58% of every dollar, for long-term care) and Paid Family & Medical Leave (about 0.81%, up to $184,500 of pay). Together they take $902 a year at this salary, leaving $53,505 (17.7%). The trade-off for all three usually shows up elsewhere (sales tax, property tax), but that's outside what a paycheck calculator can see.

The flat-rate middle: Pennsylvania, Colorado, Illinois

These three each apply a single percentage to your income, with no brackets — but they apply it to different starting points. Pennsylvania takes 3.07% of your gross wages with no deduction at all, plus a 0.07% unemployment-compensation withholding. Colorado applies 4.4% to your federal taxable income, so the federal standard deduction shrinks what gets taxed, and adds a 0.44% FAMLI paid-leave premium. Illinois applies 4.95% to your federal adjusted gross income minus a $2,925 personal exemption — it has no standard deduction, so the state with the highest rate of the three also taxes the biggest slice of your pay. At $65,000 the three come to $2,042 (Pennsylvania, 19.4%), $2,438 (Colorado, 20.0%) and $3,073 (Illinois, 21.0%) — a spread of about 1.6 percentage points of total effective tax.

New Jersey's extra layer: payroll taxes

New Jersey's income-tax brackets (1.4% to 10.75%) cost a $65,000 single filer $2,044 — only about $50 more than Pennsylvania's flat 3.07% ($1,996). What pushes New Jersey higher is a stack of small payroll taxes: unemployment insurance (0.3825%) and workforce development (0.0425%) on the first $44,800 of wages, plus temporary disability (0.19%) and family leave insurance (0.23%) on the first $171,100. At $65,000 that's $463 more, bringing New Jersey's total to $2,507 and its effective rate to 20.2%.

The progressive heavyweights: New York and California

Both states use their own multi-bracket systems on top of federal tax, and both add payroll premiums for disability and paid leave. California's mandatory 1.3% State Disability Insurance has no wage cap at all — everyone pays it, on every dollar — which is $845 at $65,000, on top of $1,975 of income tax ($2,820 in all, 20.6%). New York takes $2,913 in income tax plus $31 of SDI and $281 of Paid Family Leave premiums — $3,225 in all, the heaviest of the nine at this salary (21.3%).

California's brackets start low and climb steeply, so at $65,000 it's actually a little lighter than Illinois (20.6% vs 21.0%). That climb is why it takes over the top spot as income grows: from roughly $77,000 up, California is the heaviest of the nine.

How the ranking shifts with income

Texas and Florida are the lightest at every salary we ran, from $30,000 to $250,000, and Washington sits a steady 1.2 to 1.4 points above them. The top of the list is what moves. At $40,000 the heaviest are Illinois (18.8%) and New York (18.6%), while California is one of the lighter income-tax states (17.0%). By $100,000, California's steeper brackets put it first (27.2% vs 26.1% for New York), and the gap keeps widening — at $250,000 it's 35.6% vs 33.3%.
