The same $75,000 salary leaves you about $5,396 a year more in Nunavut than in Nova Scotia. Federal tax, CPP and EI are almost the same everywhere (Quebec is the exception), so nearly all of the difference is provincial tax. Here are the 2026 numbers for every province and territory.

Why Nunavut, BC and Alberta come out on top

Nunavut charges the least territorial tax in Canada at every salary in the table ($2,553 on $75,000, against $7,949 in Nova Scotia), so it leaves the most pay. Among the provinces, British Columbia leaves the most at $50,000 to $100,000, even after its lowest rate rose from 5.06% to 5.60% for 2026; Alberta overtakes it at $150,000 ($107,459 vs $107,377). Ontario is sixth at all four salaries: its low first rate is offset by the surtax and the Health Premium as income rises.

Federal tax is lower in Quebec because of the 16.5% federal abatement, but provincial tax is much higher. QPP costs a little more than CPP, EI costs less, and QPIP (Quebec's parental insurance) is added. The net effect at $75,000 is $2,962 less take-home pay than in Ontario.

Common questions

Which province has the highest take-home pay in Canada?

In 2026, among the provinces British Columbia leaves the most take-home pay at $50,000, $75,000 and $100,000, and Alberta at $150,000. Counting the territories, Nunavut leaves the most at every salary: $58,819 on a $75,000 salary.

Which province takes the most tax?

Nova Scotia leaves the least take-home pay at $50,000, $75,000 and $100,000 (for example $53,423 on $75,000). At $150,000, Quebec leaves the least ($97,850), with Nova Scotia next.

How much is $75,000 after tax in Ontario?

About $56,926 a year in 2026, or roughly $4,744 a month, after federal and Ontario tax (including the Ontario Health Premium), CPP, CPP2 and EI, with no other deductions.

Why is Quebec's federal tax lower?

Quebec residents get a 16.5% abatement on basic federal tax, because Quebec runs some programs the federal government funds elsewhere. Quebec's own provincial tax is higher, and workers pay QPP (instead of CPP) and QPIP, with a lower EI rate.

Do these numbers include RRSP contributions or tax refunds?

No. They are payroll estimates with only the basic personal amounts and standard payroll credits. RRSP contributions, other deductions and credits you claim when you file (such as the GST/HST credit or the Canada Workers Benefit) can change what you actually keep.
