2026 Federal Tax Brackets Explained (With a Worked Example)

Source: IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32 · 7 min read

"What tax bracket am I in?" is one of the most commonly misunderstood questions in personal finance — mostly because bracket doesn't mean what people assume it means. Here's the actual 2026 IRS schedule, what "marginal rate" really means, and a full example showing the math.

The seven 2026 brackets

These apply to taxable income — your income after the standard deduction ($16,100 single / $32,200 married filing jointly for 2026) or itemized deductions, whichever is larger.

The part everyone gets wrong: brackets are marginal, not flat

Landing in the 22% bracket does not mean 22% of your whole income goes to federal tax. It means only the slice of income that falls inside that bracket is taxed at 22% — the dollars below it were already taxed at the lower rates on the way up. Nobody's entire paycheck jumps to a higher rate just because they crossed into a new bracket.

Worked example: $100,000 salary, single filer

Start with taxable income: $100,000 minus the $16,100 standard deduction = $83,900. That amount fills up the brackets from the bottom:

  • 10% on the first $12,400 → $1,240
  • 12% on the next $38,000 (from $12,400 to $50,400) → $4,560
  • 22% on the remaining $33,500 (from $50,400 to $83,900) → $7,370

Add it up: $1,240 + $4,560 + $7,370 = $13,170 in federal tax. That's a marginal rate of 22% (the rate on the last dollar earned) but an effective rate of just 13.2% of the full $100,000 — the number that actually reflects the overall bite.

General information, not tax advice. This example uses the standard deduction only. Credits, other deductions, and additional income types can change your real result. For anything that affects your filing, confirm with a tax professional.
