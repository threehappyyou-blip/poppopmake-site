How We Verify Accuracy

Not a promise that the numbers are right — a description of the actual process used to check them, so you can judge that for yourself.

The process

  1. Every figure traces to one named primary source — a specific IRS revenue procedure or notice, a specific gov.uk/gov.scot/ato.gov.au/canada.ca page, never a second-hand summary treated as the original.
  2. Re-checked whenever there's reason to think something changed — a new tax year starting, a rate change reported in the press, or simply enough time passing that a re-check is due. There's no fixed weekly or monthly schedule; the trigger is "is there a reason to believe this might be stale," not a calendar.
  3. Cross-checked against more than one source where possible — a government page plus at least one independent secondary source (a tax firm's summary, a financial publisher's writeup), looking for agreement rather than trusting either alone.
  4. Checked against a worked example when the source provides one — the Australia calculator, for instance, was checked against the ATO's own published example (a $80,000 salary reducing to a specific take-home figure) rather than just the bracket table.
  5. Findings get logged either way — both "this changed, here's the fix" and "this was checked and is still correct" go in the changelog, not just the corrections.

What this doesn't claim

This isn't a guarantee of zero errors, and it isn't a fixed audit schedule with dates promised in advance. It's a description of the actual habit — primary sources, cross-checked, logged either way — so that if something is ever wrong, you can see exactly what standard it fell short of, rather than a vague promise that everything is fine.

See it in the calculators themselves: Every jurisdiction-based calculator now shows its source, the tax year it applies to, and when it was last checked, right at the top of the page — not buried in a footer. See the changelog for the full history, corrections included.
