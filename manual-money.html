US Paycheck Calculator

Shows what's actually left after federal tax, Social Security, Medicare, and state tax, for any of the 50 states plus D.C.

  1. Enter your salary in the amount field — the annual figure is easiest, but you can switch the frequency (see step 3) if you only know your hourly or per-paycheck rate.
  2. Choose your state from the dropdown. This matters a lot: nine states have no state income tax at all, and the rest range from a flat rate to a full progressive bracket system.
  3. Choose your pay frequency (annual, monthly, biweekly, weekly) so the "per period" figures match your actual paycheck schedule.
  4. Choose your filing status if the option is shown (single, married filing jointly, etc.) — this changes the tax brackets applied.
  5. Read the results. The breakdown shows federal tax, Social Security, Medicare, and state tax as separate line items, plus your final take-home amount for the period you chose.
Good to know: The source, tax year, and last-checked date are shown right at the top of the page in the verification box — worth a glance if you want to confirm this reflects the current tax year.

Paycheck by State

A browsing page that groups all 50 states by their tax type — no income tax, flat rate, or progressive brackets — with a link to each state's own calculator page.

  1. Open the page. It's linked from the main US Paycheck Calculator, or reachable directly.
  2. Find your state's group. States are sorted into three groups so you can see at a glance which category yours falls into.
  3. Click through to your state. Each link opens a calculator pre-set to that state, pre-filled with a short explanation of how that state's system works.
Good to know: This is a browsing convenience — the main US Paycheck Calculator already covers every state through its dropdown, so use whichever page you found first.

UK Paycheck Calculator

Shows take-home pay after Income Tax and National Insurance, correctly handling Scotland's separate tax bands.

  1. Enter your salary in the amount field.
  2. Choose your region. There's a toggle for England/Wales/Northern Ireland vs. Scotland — the two systems have genuinely different tax bands, so this choice changes your result meaningfully.
  3. Choose your pay frequency (annual, monthly, weekly) to match your actual pay schedule.
  4. Read the results. Income Tax and National Insurance are shown as separate line items, with your take-home pay for the period you chose.
Good to know: If you're not sure whether Scotland's rates apply to you, it's based on where you live, not where your employer is based.

Australia Paycheck Calculator

Shows take-home pay after federal income tax, the Low Income Tax Offset, and the Medicare levy.

  1. Enter your salary in the amount field.
  2. Choose your pay frequency to match your actual pay schedule.
  3. Read the results. Income tax, the Low Income Tax Offset, and the Medicare levy are broken out separately, since the offset and the levy's "shade-in" zone interact with the tax brackets in ways that aren't obvious from the bracket table alone.
Good to know: Australia has no state income tax, so unlike the US calculator, there's no state or territory selector needed here.

Canada Paycheck Calculator

Shows take-home pay after federal tax, provincial tax, CPP/CPP2, and EI, for any province or territory.

  1. Enter your salary in the amount field.
  2. Choose your province or territory from the dropdown — all 10 provinces and 3 territories are covered, including Quebec's separate provincial system and federal abatement.
  3. Choose your pay frequency to match your actual pay schedule.
  4. Read the results. Federal tax, provincial tax, CPP (or CPP plus the CPP2 additional contribution above a threshold), and EI are shown as separate line items.
Good to know: Quebec is genuinely different from every other province — it collects its own provincial tax separately and residents get a federal tax abatement to offset it. The calculator handles this automatically when you select Quebec.

Compare Take-Home Pay

Puts two salaries side by side — any two US states, UK regions, Australia, or Canadian provinces — to compare take-home pay and effective tax rate directly.

  1. Pick your first location and salary on the left side — choose a country/state/province and enter the salary amount.
  2. Pick your second location and salary on the right side, the same way.
  3. Read the comparison. Take-home pay and effective tax rate are shown for both sides, so you can see exactly where the difference comes from.
Good to know: This tool doesn't do currency conversion — amounts are shown exactly as entered, in each side's own currency. It's built for comparing the tax systems themselves, not for figuring out cost-of-living differences between countries.

Mortgage Calculator

Breaks down a monthly mortgage payment into principal & interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, and PMI.

  1. Enter the home price and your down payment (either as a dollar amount or a percentage — check which the field expects).
  2. Enter the interest rate and loan term (commonly 15 or 30 years).
  3. Enter property tax and insurance estimates if you have them — these vary a lot by location, so use your own numbers if you know them rather than relying on any default shown.
  4. Read the breakdown. Your total monthly payment is split into principal & interest, property tax, insurance, and PMI (if your down payment is under 20%) as separate line items.
Good to know: PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance) typically applies only when your down payment is less than 20% of the home price — if you enter a down payment at or above that, the PMI line should drop to zero.

401(k) / Retirement Calculator

Projects your 401(k) balance at retirement based on your contributions, your employer's match, and investment growth over time.

  1. Enter your current age and the age you plan to retire.
  2. Enter your current balance (if any) and your planned annual contribution.
  3. Enter your employer's match if you have one (commonly a percentage of your contribution, up to a cap).
  4. Adjust the expected annual return if you want to test different assumptions — a lower, more conservative number and a higher, more optimistic one can show you a realistic range rather than one single guess.
  5. Read the projection. Your contributions, your employer's match, and investment growth are shown as separate contributions to your final projected balance, so you can see what's actually doing the work.
Good to know: If you're between 60 and 63, check that your contribution limit reflects the higher "super catch-up" amount that age range gets — it's easy to miss since it's a narrow four-year window, higher than the ordinary 50+ catch-up on either side of it.

Capital Gains Calculator

Calculates the tax owed on a stock or crypto sale, correctly stacking the gain on top of your other income rather than taxing it in isolation.

  1. Enter your other (ordinary) income for the year — this matters a lot, since where your gain "stacks" on top of it determines the rate.
  2. Enter your proceeds (what you sold for) and your cost basis (what you originally paid).
  3. Choose short-term or long-term based on how long you held the asset (over one year is long-term, under is short-term, taxed at ordinary income rates).
  4. Choose your filing status.
  5. Read the results. Federal tax on the gain and the Net Investment Income Tax (if your income crosses the threshold) are shown separately, along with the effective rate on just the gain itself.
Good to know: The exact same dollar amount of gain can be taxed very differently depending on your other income that year — this isn't a bug, it's how a stacked, bracket-based system works. Timing a sale for a lower-income year can genuinely change the rate.

Debt Payoff Calculator

Compares the debt snowball (smallest balance first) and debt avalanche (highest interest rate first) payoff strategies side by side, for any set of debts you enter.

  1. Add each debt with its balance, interest rate, and minimum payment.
  2. Enter how much extra you can put toward debt each month, beyond the minimums.
  3. Read the comparison. Both strategies are calculated and shown side by side — total interest paid and time to become debt-free for each approach.
Good to know: The avalanche method almost always saves more in total interest, since it targets the highest interest rate first — but the snowball method's quick wins (paying off a whole small debt fast) can matter more for actually sticking with a payoff plan. There's no universally "correct" choice between them.
