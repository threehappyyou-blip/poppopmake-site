Writing & AI

All 3 tools live on one page, Writing & AI Tools, as tabs, and they share one "Your text" box at the top. None of them claims to be an "AI detector" — here is why we draw that line.

Jump to a tool
  1. AI-Cliché Check
  2. Smooth It Out

Tab 1

AI-Cliché Check

Scans your text for words and phrases that often make writing sound stock or AI-generated ("delve", "tapestry", "it's important to note that") and highlights them in context. It looks for phrases only — not grammar or spelling.

  1. Paste your text into the "Your text" box at the top of the page. It needs at least 12 words; with fewer, the page says "Paste a bit more text".
  2. Click "Analyze Text". The results appear under the "AI-Cliché Check" tab, and the line under the button says how many words it read and how many flagged phrases it found.
  3. Read the highlighted text. Each flagged phrase is highlighted in your own text in one of three colors — strong signal, promotional, or light signal (only matters in clusters). Hover over a highlight to see a suggested replacement (on a phone, where there is no hover, the "Smooth It Out" tab lists the same suggestions).
  4. Read the numbers and notes under it: words, sentences, flagged instances, and unique phrases; then notes such as an opening or closing stock phrase, unusually uniform sentence lengths, no contractions in a long text, or lots of em dashes; then a list of every flagged phrase, with ×2, ×3, … when one repeats.
  5. Edit your text and click "Analyze Text" again. The results do not update by themselves: once you change the text, a yellow note says "Text changed since this was analyzed" and the old results are dimmed until you analyze again.
Good to know: This is a pattern-matcher for known phrases, not an AI detector. It cannot prove that text was or was not written by AI: plenty of people write "robust" or "furthermore", and polished AI text can avoid every flagged word. Treat a flag as a nudge to reconsider a phrase — especially for careful, formal, or non-native English writing, which checks like this can unfairly flag. Your text stays in your browser.

Tab 2

Smooth It Out

Lists plainer replacements for the phrases found in your text. Nothing is rewritten for you.

  1. Analyze your text first: paste it into the "Your text" box and click "Analyze Text". This tab uses the same analysis as the first tab.
  2. Open the "Smooth It Out" tab. Your text appears again with the same highlights.
  3. Read the swap list under it. Each line shows a flagged phrase, an arrow, and a suggested replacement, such as "leverage → use". A ×2, ×3, … after the phrase shows how often it appeared. A suggestion like "cut it" means the phrase can usually just be deleted.
  4. Make the changes yourself in the "Your text" box — the tool never edits your text — then click "Analyze Text" again to check the result.
Good to know: Word swaps are a starting point, not the whole fix. Stiff writing is usually more about samey sentence rhythm and vague claims than about any single word, so a text can read as AI even with nothing flagged. Some formality is right for some readers, so use judgment rather than accepting every suggestion.

Tab 3

Word Count & Readability

Shows words, characters, sentences, paragraphs, reading time, and a Flesch Reading Ease score, updating live as you type — there is no button to click.

  1. Type or paste your text into the "Your text" box. Unlike the other two tabs, this one works on any amount of text, from a single word up.
  2. Open the "Word Count & Readability" tab. The numbers appear as soon as there is any text; until then it asks you to start typing.
  3. Read the five counts: words, characters (spaces included), sentences, paragraphs, and reading time (at about 225 words a minute; "<1 min" for short texts).
  4. Read the readability box. "Readability (Flesch Reading Ease)" shows a score from 0 to 100 (higher is easier), a label such as "Standard — 8th-9th grade", and an approximate US reading level underneath.
  5. Keep editing. Everything updates with each keystroke.
Good to know: The score comes from sentence length and syllable count, not meaning, and syllables are estimated, so an uncommon word can be off by one — treat the score as an estimate, not a certified grade level. The formula was built for English: for Korean, Chinese, Japanese, Cyrillic, and other non-Latin text the counts still work, but the score shows "—" and "Not available for this text" instead of a misleading number. A sentence ends at . ! ? (or 。！？); decimals like 3.14, web addresses, and abbreviations such as "Dr." do not end one, and text after the last mark still counts as a sentence.
