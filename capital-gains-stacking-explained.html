Tax on Selling Stock: The Same $50,000 Gain Can Be Taxed at 7% or 17%

2026 tax year (with 2025 for comparison) · recalculated October 2026

Long-term capital gains don't get their own separate tax bracket. They stack on top of whatever ordinary income you already have — which means the exact same gain can land in a completely different place depending on what kind of year you had.

"Stacking" in one sentence

Your capital gains brackets aren't based on your total income including the gain — they're based on where the gain starts, which is wherever your ordinary taxable income leaves off. A small gain on top of a big salary starts high on the ladder. The same gain on top of a modest salary starts near the bottom, where the long-term rate is often 0%.

Common questions

How much tax do I pay if I sell stock?

It depends on how long you held it and on your other income. Long-term gains (held more than a year) are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%, stacked on top of your other income; short-term gains are taxed at your ordinary rate of 10% to 37%. For example, a single filer with $75,000 of income pays $3,750 on a $25,000 long-term gain in 2026, or $5,500 if it was short-term.

Do I pay tax on stock gains if I make less than $50,000?

Often not on long-term gains. In 2026 a single filer pays 0% on long-term gains while taxable income (after the $16,100 standard deduction) stays at or below $49,450, so someone with $50,000 of wages can take about $15,550 of long-term gains federal-tax-free.

Do I pay tax on the whole sale price or only the gain?

Only on the gain: the sale price minus what you paid (your cost basis, including fees). Selling $30,000 of stock you bought for $20,000 is a $10,000 gain.

What is the 3.8% NIIT?

The Net Investment Income Tax adds 3.8% on investment income, including stock gains, to the extent your modified AGI is above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). These thresholds are not adjusted for inflation.

Can stock losses reduce the tax?

Yes. Losses first offset gains, and up to $3,000 of net loss a year ($1,500 if married filing separately) can offset other income; the rest carries forward. The calculator lets you enter losses and carryovers.

What this means in practice

The size of the gain isn't the only thing that determines the tax. Timing matters: realizing the same gain in a lower-income year (between jobs, a sabbatical, early retirement before other income kicks in) can genuinely mean a lower rate on the exact same sale — not a loophole, just how a stacked, bracket-based system works.
